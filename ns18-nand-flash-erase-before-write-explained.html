Why engineers designed storage that refuses to replace existing data in place

🔬 Workbench Notes

Open a file, edit it, save it — the new version replaces the old one. Simple. That's exactly how your operating system behaves. But deep inside an SSD, that isn't what happens at all. The SSD often writes your "updated" file to a completely different physical location while leaving the old copy untouched — for now.

⚡ Fast Diagnostic Summary

  • The problem: NAND flash memory cannot perform arbitrary in-place overwrites on already-programmed pages — a hardware constraint, not a design choice anyone could simply skip.
  • The severity: critical — this single constraint determines how every SSD is designed internally, from the ground up.
  • The core insight: an SSD doesn't overwrite your data. It quietly writes a new copy somewhere else, updates its internal map, and cleans up the old copy later.
  • Why overwriting is physically impossible: NAND writes in small pages but can only erase in much larger blocks — reversing a programmed cell back to its erased state requires erasing the entire surrounding block, not just one page.
  • The payoff: this one hardware rule is the direct cause of five separate systems inside every SSD — the Flash Translation Layer, garbage collection, TRIM, wear leveling, and write amplification.

Imagine writing with permanent ink instead of pencil. Once a word is written, you can't rub out a single letter — the only way to correct a mistake is to replace the entire page. Now imagine trying to run a modern operating system under those rules: every saved document, every browser cache, every log file would constantly require finding fresh pages to write on.

That sounds absurd. Yet this is remarkably close to how NAND flash memory actually works. Modern SSDs feel like ordinary disks only because an invisible layer of engineering spends every second hiding this limitation from you.

1. The First Illusion: Your Computer Thinks It Has a Disk

To Windows, Linux, or macOS, an SSD behaves like a simple block device. The operating system says "write this data to logical block 8,523," and later "replace the contents of logical block 8,523" — assuming the hardware can simply rewrite that location. The SSD knows that assumption is impossible. So the first hidden system appears: the Flash Translation Layer (FTL). The operating system sees logical addresses; the controller manages completely different physical addresses — and those two worlds almost never match directly.

2. Pages Can Be Written. Blocks Must Be Erased.

This is probably the single most important concept in the entire storage picture. NAND flash is organized roughly like this:

Die └── Plane └── Block ├── Page ├── Page ├── Page └── ...

Reading and programming happen page by page. Erasing happens block by block — two entirely different granularities. Modern page sizes are often 4 KiB, 8 KiB, or 16 KiB, and a block contains many pages, sometimes hundreds. So when one tiny page needs to become writable again, the controller cannot erase only that page — it must erase the entire block containing it. That mismatch is the beginning of every other SSD trick that follows.

3. Why Overwriting Is Impossible

Here's the subtle part many explanations skip. When a NAND block has been erased, every bit begins in the erased state (logically all 1s). Programming a page changes selected cells into programmed states (conceptually shifting 1s toward 0s). What NAND cannot do is freely reverse that process one page at a time — returning programmed cells back to the erased state requires a dedicated erase operation applied to the entire block, never an individual page. So if a page already contains data, the SSD literally cannot "edit" it in place. It needs an entirely different strategy.

4. The SSD Cheats (In the Best Possible Way)

Suppose your document changes. The operating system thinks: overwrite the existing page. Reality looks more like this:

Old Page (contains current data) ↓ Find an unused page elsewhere ↓ Write the updated data there ↓ Mark the old page as invalid ↓ Update the Flash Translation Layer

Notice what didn't happen: nothing was overwritten. The old physical page still exists — the controller simply stops pointing to it. From the operating system's perspective, nothing unusual happened. From the SSD's perspective, almost everything changed. This is one of those revelations worth sitting with for a second.

5. So When Is the Old Data Actually Erased?

Not immediately — that surprises many people. Instead, invalid pages simply accumulate. Eventually, the SSD finds a block containing a mixture of valid and invalid pages, copies the remaining valid pages elsewhere, then erases the entire block, making it available for future writes again. That background cleanup process is Garbage Collection. It isn't an optimization layered on top — it's a necessity created directly by the erase-before-write constraint.

6. One Hardware Rule Creates Five Invisible Systems

Probably the biggest payoff in this whole investigation:

NAND cannot overwrite pages ↓ The SSD needs the Flash Translation Layer ↓ Garbage Collection exists ↓ TRIM exists, to tell the SSD which logical pages are no longer needed ↓ Wear Leveling exists, to spread erase cycles across blocks ↓ Write Amplification happens, because moving valid data creates extra internal writes

One hardware limitation. An entire ecosystem of engineering built to hide it from you — and this is exactly the chain of consequences covered in more depth in why SSDs slow down when they get full, which picks up right where this one leaves off.

7. Then Why Choose NAND At All?

If NAND is this awkward, why did it replace hard drives so completely? Because the advantages are enormous: no moving parts, extremely fast random reads, low power consumption, high shock resistance, and compact manufacturing. Engineers didn't choose NAND because it was simple — they chose it because its strengths outweighed its constraints by a wide margin. The SSD controller exists largely to hide those constraints from you, and it does that job remarkably well.

8. Common Myths

  • "An SSD overwrites files the same way a hard drive does." Reality: most updates are written to a new physical page — the controller changes its internal mapping instead of rewriting the old page in place.
  • "Deleting a file immediately erases it." Reality: deletion usually just removes the logical reference. Physical erasure typically happens later, during garbage collection.
  • "TRIM makes SSDs faster." Reality: TRIM tells the SSD which logical pages are no longer needed — that information helps future garbage collection operate more efficiently, especially under sustained write workloads, rather than speeding anything up directly.
  • "The operating system knows where files physically live." Reality: it only knows logical addresses. The Flash Translation Layer constantly remaps those to changing physical flash locations.
Practical advice: understanding erase-before-write explains why SSDs benefit from keeping some free space available, why nearly full SSDs often slow down during heavy writes, why TRIM genuinely matters, why defragmentation plays a very different (and largely unnecessary) role on SSDs compared to hard drives, and why enterprise SSDs often reserve hidden spare capacity. None of these behaviors are arbitrary — they're all direct consequences of one physical rule.

9. The Bigger Lesson

Most people think an SSD is simply "a faster hard drive." It isn't. It's a sophisticated storage computer performing translation, bookkeeping, relocation, and cleanup every single time you save a file.

The operating system asks for an overwrite. The SSD quietly replies: I can't do that directly, but I can make it look like I did. That's perhaps the greatest illusion in modern storage — your computer believes it's editing data in place, while your SSD is constantly rewriting the map beneath your feet.
