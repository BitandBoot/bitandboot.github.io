🔬 Workbench Notes

You visit a weather website. It immediately suggests your city. An online store shows nearby pickup locations. You never granted GPS permission. So how did it know? Most websites don't "know" your location — they estimate it by combining multiple independent, individually weak clues.

⚡ Fast Diagnostic Summary

  • The problem: many users assume denying GPS permission completely hides their location. It doesn't — it just removes the single most precise signal from the mix.
  • The core insight: location on the web is an inference, not a single lookup. Every website already sees your public IP address; from there, geolocation databases estimate country, region, and city — usually not your exact address.
  • The browser adds more than people realize: language, time zone, and locale settings each narrow the possibilities further — a Japanese-language browser on a Japan time zone with a Japanese IP is strong evidence together, even with zero location APIs used.
  • Where GPS actually enters: browsers require explicit permission before exposing precise coordinates through the Geolocation API — denying it means the website genuinely never receives GPS data, full stop.
  • Why results vary between sites: different sites combine different signals depending on their purpose — a news site might use only IP, while a navigation app combines GPS, Wi-Fi, and cellular for street-level precision.

Imagine receiving a handwritten letter with no return address. You don't know exactly where it was written, but you notice the postage stamp, the postal routing marks, the language, the paper, the handwriting. Each clue reveals a little more. By combining enough small clues, you can often make a surprisingly accurate guess.

Websites work in much the same way. Most don't begin with your precise location. Instead, they gather a collection of weaker signals — your IP address, browser settings, language, time zone, nearby Wi-Fi networks if permission is granted, and more. Individually, none of these reveal everything. Together, they often narrow your location far more than most people realize.

1. The First Clue: Your IP Address

Every website already knows one thing: your public IP address — not because it's spying, but because that's simply how the internet sends data back to you. From that IP, IP geolocation databases can often estimate country, region, city, ISP, and organization. Usually not your house — that distinction matters. IP geolocation is probabilistic, built from network registration records and observed traffic patterns, not GPS-grade positioning.

2. The Browser Knows More Than You Think

Even without GPS permission, browsers reveal useful context: preferred language, time zone, locale, screen settings, accepted languages. None of these identify you directly. But together they narrow possibilities considerably — Japanese language, Japan time zone, and a Japanese IP address together is very strong evidence, assembled from three signals that individually reveal almost nothing.

3. When GPS Actually Enters the Picture

Here's the misconception worth clearing up directly: GPS isn't automatically available to websites. Browsers require explicit permission before exposing precise location through the Geolocation API. If permission is denied, the website simply doesn't receive GPS coordinates — there's no fallback or workaround at that point, the data genuinely isn't sent.

4. Wi-Fi Can Be Surprisingly Powerful

One of the biggest revelations here: phones and laptops can recognize nearby Wi-Fi networks, and companies maintain enormous databases mapping Wi-Fi access points to approximate physical locations. If you grant location permission, the browser may use nearby Wi-Fi — alongside GPS, cellular, or Bluetooth depending on the device — to produce a far more accurate estimate, often faster than GPS indoors where satellite signal is weak. To many readers this feels like magic. It's actually mapping.

5. Why Results Are Sometimes Wrong

VPNs, carrier-grade NAT (CGNAT), mobile networks, satellite internet, corporate networks, and recently reassigned IP addresses can all introduce errors into location databases. That's exactly why one website might think you're in Mumbai while another thinks you're in Delhi — both are working from the same imperfect signal type, just against slightly different, sometimes stale, databases.

6. Why Different Websites Know Different Amounts

A news website may only use IP. A weather app may ask for precise location. A map service may combine GPS, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and sensors. An online store may infer location from your shipping address only after you sign in. "Location" isn't one single thing a website either has or doesn't — it depends entirely on what information you've chosen to share, and what that particular site bothered to ask for.

8. Common Myths

  • "Websites always know your exact location." Reality: most only estimate it — precision depends entirely on the signals available and the permissions you've granted.
  • "An IP address reveals your home address." Reality: IP geolocation usually identifies an approximate area, not a specific residence.
  • "Blocking GPS completely hides your location." Reality: without GPS, websites can still infer an approximate location using IP and other contextual information — just with much lower precision.
  • "Every website uses the same location technology." Reality: different sites combine different signals depending on their purpose and the permissions you've granted.
Practical advice: every website necessarily sees your public IP address — that's unavoidable by design. Read location permission prompts carefully, since granting them unlocks far more precise positioning than the site had a moment before. A VPN changes your apparent network location but doesn't automatically hide every other clue — browser fingerprinting and language settings still function independently of it. If a site only needs your country, avoid granting precise location unless it's genuinely useful — navigation and ride-hailing are the honest use cases, a weather widget usually isn't.

9. The Bigger Lesson

Location on the web isn't a single switch that's either "on" or "off." It's an inference. Every signal — an IP address, a time zone, a nearby Wi-Fi network, a language setting, or GPS coordinates — adds another piece to the puzzle. Sometimes one clue is enough. More often, websites assemble several imperfect clues into a surprisingly accurate estimate.

Once you understand that, location detection stops feeling like surveillance magic. It becomes what it really is: a process of combining evidence until a reasonable conclusion emerges — the same underlying idea behind how your DNS lookups quietly reveal browsing patterns even when no single request looks alarming on its own.
